When Siblings Drift Apart — Undergraduate Thesis

Co-authored a full research thesis examining how "phubbing" (being ignored in favor of a phone) is linked to sibling relationship quality, and whether loneliness helps explain that link — a correlational study, not a claim of direct cause and effect. Owned data collection and SPSS analysis; supervised by Dr. Syeda Ayat-e-Zainab Ali, Department of Clinical Psychology, Shifa Tameer-e-Millat University.

260
participants, ages 18–30
19.6%
variance in sibling relationship quality explained by being phubbed
α ≥ 0.86
reliability across every scale used

Findings: being phubbed by a sibling was moderately linked to lower sibling relationship quality and higher loneliness, and loneliness partially explained why the two are connected. Male participants reported more phubbing and loneliness; female participants reported stronger sibling relationships overall.

Community Drug Use Patterns in Pakistan — Field Report

Research assignment for Drug Abuse Treatment and Rehabilitation, submitted to Dr. Shakir, Department of Clinical Psychology, STMU. Reviewed national survey data (UNODC, ANF) and academic literature to profile drug use patterns among a specific vulnerable community, then grounded the review in real clinical fieldwork.

Profiled substance use trends among Pathan communities in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa — where reported illicit drug use runs highest nationally — covering age, gender, and socioeconomic patterns, and the cultural context around cannabis use in the region. Supplemented the literature review with direct observations from fieldwork at Greenfield Hospital of Psychiatry, where most patients treated for drug-related psychiatric illness were young adults from the same province. Closed with concrete recommendations: better national data collection, school- and community-level prevention programs, expanded treatment access in underserved areas, and gender-responsive care models.

Perceived Social Support & Postpartum Depression — Undergraduate Thesis

Co-authored a full research thesis examining how perceived social support relates to depressive symptoms among mothers in the postpartum period, and whether perceived social support and depressive symptoms differ between mothers of a female versus a male child — a correlational, cross-sectional study. Collected data from 50 married mothers (25 with a son, 25 with a daughter), all within their first year postpartum, recruited from hospitals and public spaces in Islamabad/Rawalpindi. Owned data collection and analysis; supervised by Ms. Ayat-e-Zainab, Department of Clinical Psychology, Shifa Tameer-e-Millat University.

50
mothers surveyed — 25 with a son, 25 with a daughter
r = -.445
moderate negative correlation between perceived social support and depressive symptoms
α = .91 / .83
reliability of the Urdu-translated MSPSS and EPDS scales used

Findings: higher perceived social support was moderately linked to fewer postpartum depressive symptoms overall. However, mothers of a female child did not differ significantly from mothers of a male child in either perceived social support or depressive symptoms — a null result discussed against sample size, unmeasured demographic variables (education, socioeconomic status), and possible response bias.

Relationship Satisfaction, Love Bombing & Narcissistic Personality — SPSS Report

Full statistical analysis for a study of how narcissistic personality traits and "love bombing" relate to relationship satisfaction among university students, for the Applied Statistics and Computing (SPSS) course, submitted to Ms. Museera Aymen, Department of Clinical Psychology, STMU.

390
university students surveyed
12
distinct statistical analyses run, parametric & non-parametric
r = -0.26
entitlement/exploitativeness linked to lower relationship satisfaction

Ran descriptive statistics, reliability analysis, t-tests, one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post-hoc comparisons, Pearson correlation, regression, moderation and mediation modeling (PROCESS), plus Spearman, Mann-Whitney, Kruskal-Wallis, and chi-square tests — then wrote up every result in plain, structured language. Notable findings: men scored higher on narcissistic leadership traits than women; participants from joint family systems scored higher on narcissism than those from nuclear families; and postgraduate students reported meaningfully higher relationship satisfaction than undergraduates.
